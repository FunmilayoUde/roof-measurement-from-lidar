Roof Measurement from LiDAR Alone

An airborne laser scan records a house as a few thousand 3D points. This project turns those points into what a roofer would measure by hand: every roof surface with its pitch and true area, and every edge typed as ridge, hip, valley, eave or rake, with its length.

Oluwatifunmilayo Ude · Python, numpy/scipy, Open3D, shapely · no cloud services

01The problem

Roof work is priced from a few measurements: the area of each surface (which sets material), its pitch (which sets labour and what material is allowed), and the length of each kind of edge, because ridges, hips, valleys, eaves and rakes each take different flashing and trim. Today these come from a person on a ladder, a drone flight, or a contractor tracing an aerial photo.

The Netherlands publishes a free, nationwide laser scan, the AHN, at roughly 30–50 points per square metre. The question for this project was whether those points alone can produce the same measurements, how accurately, and where they fail.

The work was done in five stages, in order, with a measured number recorded at the end of each before moving on:

  1. Load and isolate one house from a 64-million-point tile.
  2. Find the planes: which points belong to which roof surface.
  3. Build the topology: where surfaces meet, what kind of edge each meeting is, and each surface's closed outline.
  4. Write the report: per-surface pitch and area, per-edge type and length, totals in m², ft² and roofing squares.
  5. Validate against estimates the pipeline didn't make, reported as an error distribution rather than a single mean.

Settings were never picked by eye. Each one was swept over a range, and the chosen value is the one that sits in the middle of the range that works, with the tradeoffs on either side written down.

02The data: what a laser scan is

An aircraft flies over the country with a laser that fires several hundred thousand short pulses per second at the ground, sweeping side to side. Each pulse bounces off whatever it hits and part of the light returns to a sensor beside the laser. The time between sending and receiving gives the distance: light travels about 30 cm per nanosecond, so a round trip of 6.67 microseconds means the surface was 1,000 m away.

Distance alone isn't a position. The aircraft also carries satellite positioning (GNSS) for where the scanner was, and an inertial unit (IMU) for which way it was pointing, both recorded hundreds of times a second. Scanner position plus pulse direction plus distance gives one 3D point. Repeat this across the country and you get a point cloud.

That last point matters later. A 45° roof surface covering 40 m² of ground has 56 m² of actual surface, but it receives the same number of points as 40 m² of flat ground. Points per m² of roof drop as the roof gets steeper, and any method that thinks in point counts has to account for that.

The AHN also classifies every point: ground, building, vegetation, water and so on. This project used AHN5 (2023), tile (withheld) in Delft, and kept class 6, building. Building outlines came from the BAG, the Dutch register of addresses and buildings, so each house could be cut out by its official footprint.

03Stage 1: isolating one house

The tile holds 64.8 million points. A filter over the BAG found the houses worth testing on: 700 buildings on the tile, of which 5 were single-dwelling residential, 70–300 m² in plan, and at least 3 m from any other building, so that their points wouldn't mix with a neighbour's.

Each house was cut out by its footprint plus a buffer. The buffer is needed because the footprint traces the walls, and the roof hangs past the walls by the width of the eaves. The buffer's size was measured, not guessed: grow it in steps and count the building points it captures. Once the eaves are inside, the count stops rising.

Two numbers per house carried into later stages: density and spacing. Density was measured only inside the walls, since the empty part of the buffer would otherwise dilute it. It came out at 31.6 to 49.5 points per m², with a median spacing between neighbouring points of 7–9 cm. That spacing sets the scale of everything that follows: no edge can be located much better than a few centimetres, and no surface smaller than about 1 m² has enough points to be trusted.

Coordinates were also shifted so the house sits near (0, 0). Dutch map coordinates run to about 450,000 m, and a 32-bit float, which many 3D tools use, can only resolve about 3 cm at that size. Every model and report keeps the shift alongside, so positions can be put back on the map.

04Stage 2: finding the roof planes

A roof surface is, to a good approximation, a flat plane. Stage 2 has to decide which points lie on which plane. Two standard methods were compared.

RANSAC (random sample consensus) guesses. Pick three points at random, which define a plane, and count how many other points lie within a small distance of it, the threshold. Repeat hundreds of times and keep the plane with the most supporters. Remove those points and repeat on what's left, until no plane has enough support. It needs no information beyond positions, and it's robust to stray points because they never collect support.

Region growing works locally. Estimate a direction for each point (its normal) from its neighbours, then start from a smooth spot and keep adding neighbours whose normal points the same way. It follows surfaces the way a flood fill follows a colour.

Either method on its own leaves a mess at the edges, so both were followed by the same clean-up: give each point to the plane it's closest to, merge planes that are really the same surface, drop small detached fragments, and absorb leftover points into a neighbouring plane. The order of those steps turned out to matter (see the list of fixes in section 5).

Choosing the settings

Before running stage 2 on real houses, the expected number of planes for each was written down from the stage 1 pictures alone. Two houses were unambiguous enough to serve as references: delft_02 is two flat roofs, so exactly 2 planes; delft_03 is a gable, a flat extension and a lean-to on one end, which the data showed as exactly 5 surfaces, with the two gable halves within 5% of each other in area. A setting passes only if it gets both houses right.

Each setting was then swept with the others held fixed, 172 runs in all. RANSAC also has randomness, so the two settings that mattered most, threshold and minimum plane size, were re-run as a grid over 10 random seeds.

The tradeoffs are physical. A smaller threshold rejects points that are genuinely on the roof, because the scanner's own noise is about 3 cm; at 0.04 m a third of some roofs went unassigned. A larger threshold lets a plane steal points from its neighbour near the shared edge, and near the ridge of a low-pitched roof a single near-flat plane can swallow both slopes. The minimum size trades missed small surfaces (delft_03's 2.5 m² lean-to disappears at 3 m²) against fragments counted as surfaces (below 1 m²).

Why RANSAC won. Region growing has real strengths. On a synthetic shallow hip roof at a large threshold it found all four surfaces where RANSAC grabbed a flat slab across the ridge. But on real houses, every setting that fixed one reference broke the other. Since that synthetic failure hasn't appeared on a real house yet, RANSAC was carried forward, and region growing's normals are kept as an independent visual check.

05Stage 3: edges and outlines

Planes alone aren't a roof model. A plane goes on forever; a roof surface stops at its edges. Stage 3 finds those edges and closes each surface into a polygon.

Internal edges are where two surfaces meet, and they come from geometry rather than from the points. Two non-parallel planes always meet in a straight line, which can be computed exactly from their equations. The points only say where along that line the edge runs: the stretch where both surfaces have points close by. Where three planes meet, as at the end of a hip roof's ridge, the corner is the single point common to all three, again exact. On synthetic roofs these three-plane corners came out within about 1 cm.

The type of an internal edge depends on which way the two surfaces fall away from it. If both slope down and away, it's a ridge (or a hip when it runs diagonally down to a corner). If both slope up and away, water collects along it: a valley. If one is flat and the other sloped, it's a change of pitch, here called a break. The test looks at the points of each surface just beside the edge, because a surface with a notch in it can have its centre on the wrong side of its own edge.

Outer edges are harder, because nothing meets them. The points simply stop. The method traces each surface's outline (an alpha shape: a "shrink-wrap" around the points that can follow concave corners), splits it into straight runs, and labels each run by what it touches: a neighbouring surface, or nothing. Runs that touch nothing become outer lines, snapped to level (an eave) or straight up the slope (a rake) when they're within 10° of it. Each surface's polygon is then the intersection of its internal and outer lines.

One correction makes a measurable difference. The outermost laser point along an edge is never exactly on the edge: points are about 9 cm apart, so the last one sits on average part of a spacing inside. Placing the line through the outermost point made every surface read 1–3% small. The line is instead placed at a slightly inner point plus the gap expected for that density and edge length, which also ignores a few stray points beyond the edge. That took the median area error on synthetic roofs from about −1% to within ±0.1%.

What broke, and how it was fixed

Most of the work in stage 3 was finding failures. Each of these was found by a test or by a number that didn't add up, and each fix was kept only if every earlier test still passed.

06Stage 4: the measurement report

The report is a JSON file per house. Each physical edge is listed once, even though two surfaces share it. Areas are true (sloped) areas, also given in ft² and roofing squares (one square is 100 ft²), and pitch is also given as rise per 12 units of run, as US roofers quote it. Each surface carries a confidence and an error bar taken from the synthetic tests in section 7.

07Verification on synthetic roofs

A roof generated in code has a known answer, so a method can be graded on it exactly. Gable, hip, T-shaped (cross-gable) and dormer roofs were generated at the real density (33 points/m²) with 3 cm of height noise, 20 random versions of each, and put through the full pipeline.

The code has 106 automated tests. These include checks that each roof yields exactly the right number of edges of each type, that edge lengths match the true ones within 0.3 m, and that the 3D model's mesh area matches the report's.

This chart is the reason the report gives an error distribution by surface size rather than one accuracy figure. The large surfaces, which hold most of the area, are measured well. The small ones are not, and a single average would hide that.

08Validation on real houses

Synthetic roofs are perfect planes with perfect edges. Real roofs have gutters, chimneys, sagging, moss and missing points. Two checks were set up that the pipeline can't grade itself on.

Check 1: footprint and pitch

If you know a house's footprint and its roof pitch, you know its roof area: a surface at pitch θ covering ground area A has true area A / cos θ. The footprint comes from the BAG, which the pipeline never uses for measuring. The textbook version of this check turned out to be wrong in two ways, and both were worth finding.

A: footprint / cos(mean pitch)  →  B: footprint × mean(1 / cos pitch)  →  D: (footprint + overhang) × mean(1 / cos pitch)

The mean of a curve isn't the curve of the mean. 1/cos θ bends upward, so for a roof with mixed pitches, 1/cos of the average pitch is smaller than the average of 1/cos. On delft_03 (a 43° gable plus a flat extension) the first gives 1.142 and the second 1.252: a 9% difference from averaging alone. Estimate B averages correctly.

The footprint is the walls, not the roof. The roof extends past the walls by the eaves. Widening the footprint uniformly (C) overshoots by 6–13%, because gable ends and flat roofs barely overhang. Estimate D measures the overhang directly: the number of roof points outside the footprint divided by the point density gives the overhang's plan area.

This check uses the report's own pitches and its split between surfaces, so it tests consistency, not correctness. A pipeline that got every pitch wrong in the same way could still pass. That's why the second check exists.

Check 2: hand measurements

Pending

The protocol: open each house's points in CloudCompare and pick two points per edge and per slope, twice each and independently, before looking at the report, so the answer can't pull the measurement towards it. Plan lengths can also be measured on the PDOK aerial photo, a different sensor on a different date. The comparison script then reports each kind of measurement as a distribution (median, spread, worst case), and the difference between a person's own repeats sets the smallest error the check can detect.

09Limitations and next steps

  • Small surfaces. Dormers and other surfaces under 10 m² have a median area error of 23% and a 90th percentile of 32% on synthetic roofs. Three attempted fixes each helped the dormer and broke something else, and were backed out. A dedicated dormer model is the next step, designed around real dormers.
  • Four houses, one tile. All the real results come from four houses in one part of Delft, and the settings were chosen on two of them. None of them has a valley or a dormer. A second area, Bilthoven, has been scouted with 222 detached candidates (160 of them L- or T-shaped), but its download service has been down.
  • Curved roofs. delft_01's top isn't planar: it has two humps about 0.25 m high. A plane-based method slices it into strips. It is kept as a known hard case and its traced pieces are marked low confidence.
  • No independent ground truth yet. The footprint check is consistency only. The hand measurements are the missing piece.
  • Classification is trusted. Points the AHN labels as building are taken as the roof. An unlabelled chimney leaves a hole; a mislabelled shed would be measured.
